## Salesforce AI Research took 21 papers to ICLR 2026

_Gap-fill: **ICLR 2026 ran April 23–27, 2026** in Rio de Janeiro. Outside the scan window, unrecorded in this radar, and the best available map of where Salesforce's agent capability comes from next._

Salesforce AI Research had **21 papers accepted to ICLR 2026** — the Fourteenth International Conference on Learning Representations, one of the three top-tier machine-learning venues alongside NeurIPS and ICML. Acceptance there is peer-reviewed and competitive, which is what separates it from a vendor whitepaper. The accepted work clusters into four themes Salesforce named explicitly: **LLM reasoning**, **evaluation systems**, **knowledge graphs**, and **agent architectures**. Separately, work on **agent identity failures** — how an LLM agent drifts away from its assigned role when left to interact autonomously over many turns — was accepted to the **Agents in the Wild** workshop at the same conference.

**How to read this as a practitioner.** Do not read 21 papers. Read the *shape* of the portfolio, because it is a reliable 12-to-24-month preview of what shows up in the product. Two of the four themes — evaluation systems and agent architectures — are the same two that already surfaced as shipped artefacts in the last week's scans (`agentforce-adlc`'s adversarial testing, CRMArena, GIFT-Eval). That consistency is the signal: **this organisation's default move is to build the thing that proves an agent is wrong before building the agent.** The agent-identity-drift work is the one to watch most closely if you deploy multi-agent systems, because role drift over a long session is precisely the failure mode that Multi-Agent Orchestration makes more likely and that a single-turn test will never catch. Knowledge graphs is the theme with the clearest product line of sight — it sits directly under Data 360's Intelligent Context, which chunks and vectorises unstructured content into a searchable knowledge graph.

**Status:** Published research, peer-reviewed and presented April 2026. Not a product commitment — none of it is guaranteed to ship.

## HAS-Bench is not a Salesforce benchmark

This entry exists because the scan nearly got it wrong, and the same trap is waiting for anyone researching this area. **HAS-Bench** — *"Evaluating LLM-Based Human-Agent Systems under Configurable Human Participation"* ([arXiv 2607.04329](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.04329), July 2026) — surfaces high in searches for recent Salesforce agent-evaluation work, and its subject matter sits squarely in Salesforce's territory: a graph-based framework where humans are modelled as first-class nodes alongside LLM agents, with explicit roles, permissions, communication paths and action authority, evaluated on both task outcomes and process behaviour (clarification quality, feedback use, control calibration, safety, initiative, interaction cost). On verification, its authors are affiliated with **the University of Tokyo, the University of Illinois Chicago, MBZUAI, McGill University and Zhejiang University** — **not Salesforce**.

**Why bother recording a negative.** Two reasons, both practical. First, benchmark attribution is exactly the kind of detail that gets repeated without checking and then quoted in a design review or an interview, where being wrong is expensive. Salesforce's actual agent-evaluation line is **CRMArena / CRMArena-Pro**, **SCUBA** and **GIFT-Eval** — that is the list to memorise. Second, the paper is genuinely worth reading on its own merits, and its central finding is directly useful: human participation substantially improves task completion and failure recovery, **but the size of the gain depends on when, how and by whom the human intervenes**. That is the empirical case for designing hand-off points deliberately rather than bolting on a generic "escalate to human" action — which is the default, and the weakest, pattern in most Agentforce builds.

**Status:** Third-party academic preprint, July 2026. Not Salesforce research; not a product.
